What's happening inside your muscles?

01 / Recruitment and rate coding

In class, we learned that a motor unit consists of an alpha motor neuron and all the muscle fibers it innervates. When lifting a lighter weight, smaller motor units are generally recruited first. As the weight gets heavier, larger motor units are recruited to help produce more force. This is known as the size principle. The nervous system can also increase force by increasing how frequently motor neurons fire, which is called rate coding (Henneman et al.; Heckman and Enoka). In the demonstration, you can see how recruitment changes when you select a heavier weight.

02 / Muscle fiber types

Another concept we covered in class was the difference between Type I and Type II muscle fibers. Type I fibers contract more slowly and are more resistant to fatigue, while Type II fibers contract faster and can produce more power. Type IIa fibers are generally more resistant to fatigue than Type IIx fibers. Both types can contribute during exercise, but heavier weights generally require the recruitment of larger motor units (Heckman and Enoka). This helps explain why lifting a heavy weight feels different from lifting a light one.

03 / Central and peripheral fatigue

As we learned when discussing fatigability, there are different reasons why our ability to produce force decreases during exercise. Central fatigue occurs when the nervous system becomes less able to fully activate the muscles. Peripheral fatigue involves changes at the neuromuscular junction or within the muscle itself that reduce force production (Gandevia). In the demonstration, performing more repetitions decreases your available force. This represents performance fatigability, which is a decline in performance during a task (Enoka and Duchateau).

04 / Recovery

Rest gives the nervous system and muscles time to recover some of their ability to produce force. This is why taking a break between sets can help us perform more repetitions. The amount of recovery needed depends on the exercise and its intensity and duration (Gandevia; Enoka and Duchateau). By pressing the rest button, you can see how recovery helps restore force capacity.

Project application & context

Connection to neuromuscular aspects of exercise

I wanted to show how the concepts we learned in APK4115 apply to exercises that many of us perform at the gym. By changing the weight, performing repetitions and resting, you can see how motor unit recruitment, rate coding, muscle fiber types and performance fatigability relate to one another. The main idea is to show how the nervous system and muscles work together to produce force and how that changes as we fatigue.

Why I chose this topic and medium

I chose this topic because I enjoy weightlifting and wanted to connect what we have learned throughout the semester to something I do regularly. I thought making an interactive demonstration would be a fun way to show these concepts because instead of just reading about fatigue, you can change the weight, perform repetitions and see what happens when you rest. I used AI assistance to help build the website and animations.

Model limitations

The indicators are simplified examples of what happens during exercise, not actual measurements of muscle activity or fatigue. They do not predict how many repetitions someone can perform. The fatigue indicator does not measure central and peripheral fatigue separately. Each exercise starts fresh.
